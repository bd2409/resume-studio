Changing careers after 40 is more common than it looks. Industries change, roles disappear, priorities shift. The professionals who switch successfully rarely start from zero. They make a deliberate, adjacent move and tell a clear story about it.

## Step 1: Choose an adjacent move (weeks 1 and 2)

There are three kinds of career change:

- **Same function, new industry**: a finance controller moving from manufacturing to a SaaS company.
- **Same industry, new function**: a sales head moving into business development or partnerships.
- **New function and new industry**: the hardest, and the slowest.

The first two let you keep most of your credibility. Write down three possible target roles and, for each, how much of your current experience carries over. Choose the one with the highest overlap that still excites you.

## Step 2: Map transferable skills (week 2)

List what you've done in the last ten years under four headings:

1. **People**: teams led, stakeholders managed, hiring, training.
2. **Money**: budgets, P&L, cost savings, revenue.
3. **Process**: systems built, improvements, compliance, launches.
4. **Domain**: industry knowledge, customers, regulations, tools.

Then read 10 job descriptions for your target role and mark which of their requirements you already meet. The unmarked ones are your gaps.

## Step 3: Close the top gaps with proof (weeks 3 to 10)

Pick the two or three gaps that appear in most postings. For each, aim for **evidence**, not just a certificate:

- A project at your current job that uses the new skill.
- A short freelance or volunteer project.
- A course with a practical capstone you can describe in an interview.

AI tools such as Claude can speed this up: use them to learn a new domain quickly, draft analyses, and practise interviews. Being visibly fluent with AI also answers the unspoken question of whether an experienced hire will adapt.

## Step 4: Reposition your story (weeks 8 to 10)

- **Resume**: a headline with the new target role, a summary that connects your past results to the new role, and bullets reordered to show transferable achievements first.
- **LinkedIn**: a new headline, an About section that explains the move confidently, and posts or comments about your new field.
- **Your 60-second pitch**: "For 15 years I've done X, with results like Y. I'm now moving into Z because..., and here's what I bring."

## Step 5: Use your network (weeks 10 to 13)

After 40, most good opportunities come through people. Reconnect with 20 to 30 former colleagues, clients and classmates. Ask for advice and introductions, not jobs. Apply to fewer, better-matched roles with a tailored resume for each.

## Common mistakes

- Trying to switch function **and** industry at once.
- Quitting before the new direction is tested.
- Collecting certificates without real projects.
- Applying with the old resume and hoping recruiters connect the dots.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is 40 too late for a career change in India?
No. Many professionals change careers in their 40s, especially through adjacent moves that reuse their experience. The key is a specific target role and a clear story.

### Should I take a pay cut to change careers?
Sometimes a lateral move or a small cut is the price of entry into a new field, but adjacent moves often avoid it. Decide your minimum acceptable package before you start interviewing.

### Do I need an MBA to change careers after 40?
Usually not. Practical evidence of the new skills, a strong network and a well-positioned resume matter more for most mid-career moves.

### How can I explain a career change in an interview?
Keep it forward-looking: what you've achieved, what draws you to the new role, and how your experience will help them. Avoid criticising your past employer or field.
