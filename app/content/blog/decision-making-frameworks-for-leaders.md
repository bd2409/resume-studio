Most teams don't suffer from bad decisions as much as from **slow** or **unclear** ones: nobody knows who decides, meetings end without a call, and reversible choices get treated like bet-the-company moves. These frameworks fix that.

## 1. One-way and two-way doors: set the speed

Jeff Bezos described two kinds of decisions in his 2015 letter to Amazon shareholders:

- **One-way doors** are hard or impossible to reverse: a large acquisition, shutting a business line, a senior hire. Decide carefully and consult widely.
- **Two-way doors** can be reversed: a pricing test, a new process, a campaign. Decide quickly, with a small group, and learn from the result.

Most decisions are two-way doors. Treating them as one-way doors is how organisations slow down.

## 2. RAPID: who does what in a decision

Developed by Bain & Company, RAPID names the roles:

- **Recommend**: gathers input and proposes a decision.
- **Agree**: must sign off (use sparingly, for example legal or compliance).
- **Perform**: carries out the decision.
- **Input**: consulted for facts and views.
- **Decide**: makes the final call. One person.

## 3. DACI: a simpler version for projects

- **Driver**: runs the process and gets the decision made.
- **Approver**: makes the decision (one person).
- **Contributors**: provide expertise.
- **Informed**: told the outcome.

Write the DACI at the top of any decision document. It ends the "who's deciding this?" debate.

## 4. The OODA loop: decide in fast-moving situations

John Boyd's loop: **Observe** what's happening, **Orient** (make sense of it), **Decide**, **Act**, and repeat. The team that loops faster than its competitor or the crisis wins. Use it during a launch, an outage or a market shock.

## 5. Cynefin: choose the right approach

Dave Snowden's Cynefin framework sorts situations into domains:

| Situation | What it looks like | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Clear | Cause and effect are obvious | Apply best practice |
| Complicated | Experts can work out the answer | Analyse, then act |
| Complex | Cause and effect are only clear in hindsight | Run small experiments, amplify what works |
| Chaotic | No clear cause and effect, urgent | Act to stabilise, then reassess |

A common mistake is treating a complex problem (entering a new market) as complicated (one big analysis and plan).

## 6. The pre-mortem: find risks before they happen

Psychologist Gary Klein's technique: before committing, imagine it's a year later and the decision has **failed**. Each person writes down why. You surface risks that people would otherwise keep to themselves.

## 7. 10/10/10: keep emotion in proportion

From writer Suzy Welch: how will I feel about this decision in **10 minutes**, **10 months** and **10 years**? Useful for hard people decisions and career moves.

## A simple decision process

1. Is it a one-way or two-way door?
2. Who decides (DACI or RAPID)?
3. What kind of situation is it (Cynefin)?
4. For big decisions, run a pre-mortem.
5. Decide, write down the reasoning, and set a date to review.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best decision-making framework for managers?
For everyday use, combine one-way and two-way doors (to set the speed) with DACI or RAPID (to make roles clear). Add Cynefin and pre-mortems for larger, uncertain decisions.

### What's the difference between RAPID and DACI?
Both clarify roles in a decision. RAPID, from Bain, adds an "Agree" role for mandatory sign-offs and separates who performs the decision. DACI is simpler and common for project decisions.

### How do I make decisions faster as a leader?
Treat reversible decisions as two-way doors, give one person the authority to decide, set a deadline, and accept that some decisions will be corrected later.

### How do I talk about decision-making in an interview?
Use a STAR story: the situation, the decision you owned, the framework or process you used, and the measurable result.
