## Do you need a decision framework?

You probably do if:

- Meetings end with "let's discuss again next week".
- Nobody is sure who has the final say.
- Small, reversible choices get the same scrutiny as big bets.
- Big decisions go wrong for reasons someone saw coming but didn't say.

## Consensus feels safe. Clarity is faster.

Many teams aim for everyone to agree. Agreement is nice, but it isn't required. What a team needs is a clear decider, a fair hearing for input, and the commitment to act once the call is made, even by those who disagreed.

## 1. One-way and two-way doors

- **One-way doors:** hard to reverse (an acquisition, closing a business line, a senior hire). Decide carefully and consult widely.
- **Two-way doors:** reversible (a pricing test, a new process, a campaign). Decide quickly with a small group.

**Good to know:** treating two-way doors as one-way doors is one of the biggest causes of slow organisations.

**Best for:** any team that feels it moves too slowly.

## 2. RAPID

- **Recommend:** gathers input and proposes.
- **Agree:** must sign off (use sparingly, for example legal).
- **Perform:** carries it out.
- **Input:** consulted for facts and views.
- **Decide:** makes the call. One person.

**Good to know:** too many "Agree" roles turn RAPID back into consensus.

**Best for:** large, cross-functional decisions.

## 3. DACI

- **Driver:** runs the process.
- **Approver:** decides (one person).
- **Contributors:** provide expertise.
- **Informed:** told the outcome.

**Good to know:** write the DACI at the top of the decision document. It ends the "who's deciding this?" debate.

**Best for:** project and product decisions.

## 4. OODA loop

**Observe, Orient, Decide, Act**, then repeat. The team that learns faster than the situation changes wins. Use it during a launch, an outage or a market shock.

## 5. Cynefin

| Situation | What it looks like | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Clear | Cause and effect are obvious | Apply best practice |
| Complicated | Experts can work out the answer | Analyse, then act |
| Complex | Cause and effect only clear in hindsight | Run small experiments |
| Chaotic | Urgent, no clear pattern | Act to stabilise, then reassess |

A common mistake is treating a complex problem, such as entering a new market, as a complicated one that a big analysis can solve.

## 6. Pre-mortem

Before committing, imagine it's a year later and the decision has **failed**. Everyone writes down why. You surface risks people would otherwise keep to themselves.

## 7. 10/10/10

How will I feel about this in **10 minutes**, **10 months** and **10 years**? Useful for hard people decisions and career moves.

## How to make a decision in one meeting

1. Ask: one-way or two-way door?
2. Name the decider (DACI or RAPID).
3. Identify the situation type (Cynefin).
4. For one-way doors, run a 15-minute pre-mortem.
5. Decide, and write down the reasoning.
6. Set a date to review the result.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best decision-making framework for managers?
For everyday use, combine one-way and two-way doors (to set the speed) with DACI or RAPID (to make roles clear). Add Cynefin and pre-mortems for larger, uncertain decisions.

### What's the difference between RAPID and DACI?
Both clarify roles. RAPID, from Bain, adds an "Agree" role for mandatory sign-offs and separates who performs the decision. DACI is simpler and common for project decisions.

### How do I make decisions faster as a leader?
Treat reversible decisions as two-way doors, give one person the authority to decide, set a deadline, and accept that some decisions will be corrected later.

### What is a pre-mortem?
A pre-mortem is a short exercise where the team imagines a decision has failed and writes down the reasons, so risks can be addressed before committing.

### What is the OODA loop?
OODA stands for Observe, Orient, Decide, Act. It's a cycle for acting quickly in changing situations, developed by military strategist John Boyd.

### How do I talk about decision-making in an interview?
Use a STAR story: the situation, the decision you owned, the framework or process you used, and the measurable result.
