At senior levels, interviews are less about whether you can do the work and more about whether you'll fit, adapt and lead. Here are the questions experienced professionals get most often, and how to answer them.

## 1. "Tell me about yourself."

Use a 60 to 90 second answer in three parts:

- **Present**: your current role, scope and one headline result.
- **Past**: two career moments that built the skills this role needs.
- **Future**: why this role, now.

> "I lead supply chain for a consumer goods company across 4 plants, where we cut logistics cost by 15% in two years. Before that I spent eight years in procurement and plant operations, so I understand both sides of the cost equation. I'm now looking for a role where I can do that at a national scale, which is why this position stood out."

## 2. "Aren't you overqualified for this role?"

Address the hidden worries: that you'll be bored, expensive or leave soon.

> "I can see why you'd ask. What attracts me is [specific aspect of the role]. My experience means I'll get up to speed quickly and avoid mistakes I've seen before. I'm looking for a place to contribute for the long term, and this role fits that."

## 3. "Why are you leaving after so many years?"

Stay forward-looking and never criticise your employer.

> "I've learned a great deal there, and I've delivered what I set out to. I'm now looking for [bigger scope, a new industry, a growth-stage company], and this role offers exactly that."

## 4. "How do you keep your skills current?"

Give a specific, recent example. Mentioning how you use AI tools well is a strong answer today.

> "Last quarter I started using Claude to prepare our weekly operations review. It cut preparation from four hours to under one, and the team now uses the same template."

## 5. "Tell me about a time you led a team through change."

Use STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) with numbers: the size of the team, what changed, how you brought people along and the result.

## 6. "Describe a failure and what you learned."

Choose a real but recoverable mistake, own it, and spend most of the answer on what you changed afterwards.

## 7. "How would you approach your first 90 days here?"

Listen, learn and deliver one early win: meet key stakeholders, understand the numbers, identify two or three priorities, and agree on them with your manager.

## 8. "What are your salary expectations?"

Give a researched range based on the role's scope, and stay flexible on structure.

> "Based on the scope we've discussed, I'm looking at Rs X to Y lakh fixed, and I'm open on how the package is structured."

## Questions you should ask them

- What would make someone exceptional in this role after a year?
- What's the biggest challenge the team faces right now?
- How will success be measured in the first 90 days?
- Why is this role open?

## Frequently asked questions

### How should experienced candidates prepare for interviews?
Prepare a strong introduction, five STAR stories with numbers, answers to the questions above, and thoughtful questions about the role. Practise out loud, ideally with a mock interviewer.

### How do I answer "Why should we hire you?" as a senior candidate?
Connect two or three of your most relevant results to the role's biggest needs, and explain how your experience reduces risk and speeds up impact for them.

### Should I bring up my age in an interview?
No. Focus on your results, adaptability and motivation. If concerns come up indirectly, address them with evidence of recent learning and energy for the role.

### How long should interview answers be?
Most answers work best at 60 to 120 seconds. Use STAR to stay structured and stop once you've given the result.
