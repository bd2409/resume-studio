## The Ladder of Inference at a glance

| Rung | What happens | Example | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Observable data | Everything that happened | The full meeting | What happened, as a camera would record it? |
| 2. Selected data | What you noticed | "She was 10 minutes late" | What did I leave out? |
| 3. Meanings | What you think it means | "She didn't prioritise this" | What else could it mean? |
| 4. Assumptions | What you assume | "She doesn't value my project" | What am I assuming? |
| 5. Conclusions | What you decide | "She's not committed" | Is this the only conclusion? |
| 6. Beliefs | What you now believe | "People in her team never deliver" | Is this belief based on enough evidence? |
| 7. Actions | What you do | You leave her out of the next meeting | What would I do if I were wrong? |

## Do you need the Ladder of Inference?

It's useful when:

- You find yourself certain about someone's motives.
- Two people describe the same meeting completely differently.
- A team keeps repeating the same disagreement.
- You're giving feedback and want to stick to facts.

## The problem isn't drawing conclusions. It's forgetting they're conclusions.

We have to climb the ladder to function; nobody can analyse every detail. The danger is the **reflexive loop**: our beliefs decide which data we notice next, so we keep finding proof for what we already think.

## 1. Climb down: check your data

Before reacting, go back to rung one. What did you actually see or hear?

**Good to know:** writing the facts down in one or two lines often shows how little data a strong conclusion rests on.

**Best for:** moments when you feel very sure, very fast.

## 2. Test your assumptions

List the assumptions behind your conclusion, and ask whether each one is true.

> "I'm assuming she knew the meeting was important. Did anyone tell her?"

**Good to know:** asking "What else could explain this?" and finding two other explanations breaks the loop.

**Best for:** judgements about people's motives.

## 3. Make your reasoning visible

In conversations, share how you got to your view: "Here's what I saw, here's what I took it to mean, here's what I concluded. Does that match what happened?"

**Good to know:** this pairs well with [SBI feedback](/blog/feedback-and-coaching-frameworks-for-managers), which keeps you on the bottom rungs.

**Best for:** feedback and disagreements.

## 4. Ask others to walk down their ladder

"What did you see that led you there?" invites people to share their data without feeling attacked.

## How to use the ladder in your next difficult conversation

1. Before the conversation, write down the facts you observed.
2. Mark which parts are facts and which are your interpretation.
3. Open with the facts, not the conclusion.
4. Share your interpretation as a possibility, not a verdict.
5. Ask for their view and listen.
6. Agree what you both now understand and what happens next.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the Ladder of Inference?
It's a model, developed by Chris Argyris, that shows how people move from observable data to beliefs and actions through a series of quick mental steps, often without noticing.

### What are the steps of the Ladder of Inference?
Observable data, selected data, meanings, assumptions, conclusions, beliefs and actions.

### What is the reflexive loop?
It's the way our existing beliefs influence which data we notice next, so we keep finding evidence that confirms what we already think.

### How do you use the Ladder of Inference at work?
Before reacting, go back to the facts, test your assumptions, share your reasoning openly and ask others how they reached their view.

### Who popularised the Ladder of Inference?
Peter Senge popularised it in his 1990 book The Fifth Discipline, building on Chris Argyris's work.

### How is it related to the Johari Window?
The Johari Window helps you understand how others see you; the Ladder of Inference helps you check how you see others. Together they improve self-awareness and communication.
