By Bhargab Das, marketing strategist for startups.

Strategy is about **choices**: who you serve, what you offer, where you compete and what you won't do. These frameworks help founders and leaders make those choices with clarity. The test of each one is simple: did it change a decision?

## 1. SWOT: where you stand

List your **Strengths** and **Weaknesses** (internal) and **Opportunities** and **Threats** (external). The value comes from the next step: how will you use strengths to grab opportunities, and fix weaknesses that expose you to threats?

## 2. Porter's Five Forces: how attractive is this market?

Michael Porter's model looks at five pressures on profits:

1. Rivalry among existing competitors
2. Threat of new entrants
3. Bargaining power of buyers
4. Bargaining power of suppliers
5. Threat of substitutes

Use it before entering a new market or category. A market where buyers can switch easily and substitutes are everywhere needs a very sharp edge to be profitable.

## 3. Ansoff matrix: how will you grow?

| | Existing products | New products |
|---|---|---|
| **Existing markets** | Market penetration (lowest risk) | Product development |
| **New markets** | Market development | Diversification (highest risk) |

Most startups should win penetration first: sell more of what works to the customers who already want it.

## 4. STP: who exactly are you for?

**Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning.** Divide the market into groups, choose the one you can win, and position yourself clearly for them.

> Segment: D2C skincare brands doing Rs 1 to 10 crore a year.
> Target: founders who run marketing themselves.
> Positioning: "The marketing team you can't hire yet."

## 5. Jobs to Be Done: why do customers really buy?

Associated with Clayton Christensen: customers "hire" a product to get a job done in their life. Ask what progress they're trying to make, not what features they want. A working professional buying a resume service isn't buying a document; they're hiring it to get interviews for a better job, quickly and without embarrassment.

## 6. The 4Ps: shape the offer

**Product, Price, Place, Promotion.** A quick check that the offer is consistent: a premium product sold at discount prices through the wrong channel will confuse customers.

## 7. The flywheel: how growth compounds

Popularised by Jim Collins: a loop where each step pushes the next. For example: better product, happier customers, more reviews and referrals, lower acquisition cost, more money to invest in the product. Draw your flywheel and invest in the step that's slowest.

## Putting them together

1. **Who**: STP and Jobs to Be Done.
2. **Where**: Porter's Five Forces and the Ansoff matrix.
3. **What**: the 4Ps.
4. **How it compounds**: the flywheel.
5. **Check your position** every six months with a SWOT.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the most important strategy frameworks for a startup?
STP and Jobs to Be Done (to pick and understand your customer), the Ansoff matrix (to plan growth) and the flywheel (to see how growth compounds). SWOT and Porter's Five Forces help with periodic reviews and new markets.

### Is SWOT analysis still useful?
Yes, as a quick summary, but only if it leads to specific actions. A SWOT that just lists points doesn't change anything.

### What is the Jobs to Be Done framework?
It's the idea that customers "hire" products to make progress in a specific situation. Understanding that job helps you design, position and price your product.

### What's the difference between strategy and marketing frameworks?
Strategy frameworks such as Five Forces and Ansoff help decide where to compete. Marketing frameworks such as STP and the 4Ps help decide how to win customers there. Founders need both.
