The 35+ Career Comeback Playbook

For {{ reader.name or 'you' }}, from {{ cfg.OWNER_NAME }}. Read it once, then keep it open while you work through the 30-day plan at the end.

1. Your experience is the advantage

After 35 you bring what younger candidates can't: judgement from real decisions, a network, depth in your domain, and a record of results. Companies pay for that. When experienced people struggle to get hired, it's rarely because of age itself. It's usually two gaps that hide their value:

  • The visibility gap. A resume and LinkedIn profile that read like 2010: long duty lists, old roles in full detail, no clear story of the impact you deliver today.
  • The AI-fluency gap. Hiring managers quietly wonder whether a senior hire will adapt to new tools. Showing that you already use AI well answers that question before it's asked.

This playbook closes both.

2. Seven fixes that stop your resume looking dated

  1. Show the last 15 years in detail. List older roles in one line each (company, title), or group them under "Earlier career".
  2. Drop graduation years for degrees more than 15 years old. The degree and institution are enough.
  3. Replace the "career objective" with a 3 to 4 line summary: years of experience, your domain, two or three quantified results, and the role you're targeting.
  4. Lead every bullet with a result, not a duty. "Cut vendor costs by 18% across 40 sites" beats "Responsible for vendor management".
  5. Keep it to two pages in a clean, single-column format that applicant tracking systems can read. No photos, tables or graphics.
  6. Update your skills list. Remove tools nobody uses anymore. Add current ones you genuinely use, including AI tools.
  7. Modernise the details: a professional Gmail or own-domain email, your LinkedIn URL, and city only (no full address, date of birth or marital status).

3. LinkedIn checklist for senior professionals

  • A recent, professional photo and a simple banner that states what you do.
  • A headline that names your target role and two specialisms, not just your current title.
  • An About section in first person: the problems you solve, three proof points, and what you're looking for.
  • Your last 3 roles written as achievements; older roles kept brief.
  • Skills ordered for the role you want, with your top 3 pinned.
  • Post or comment once a week on your field. Experience shared publicly is the strongest signal you're current.

4. Getting started with Claude

Claude is an AI assistant made by Anthropic. Open claude.ai, sign up with your email (there's a free plan) and type to it like you'd brief a smart colleague. Three habits make the answers far better:

  • Give context: who you are, who it's for, and what good looks like.
  • Paste the material: your resume, the job description, the report, the email thread.
  • Iterate: "shorter", "more confident", "make it sound like me", "give me three options".

Always check facts and numbers before you use anything it writes. You stay the expert; Claude does the heavy lifting.

7. Your first AI project in 60 minutes

  1. Pick one task you repeat every week that takes over an hour: a status report, a sales summary, a hiring shortlist, a customer reply.
  2. Write the brief (10 minutes): what the task is, who it's for, what a great result looks like, and one past example.
  3. Run it in Claude (20 minutes) with this week's material. Iterate until it's something you'd send.
  4. Save the prompt as a reusable template (in a Claude Project, or a document) so next week takes 10 minutes.
  5. Measure it: time saved, quality, errors caught. That's now a resume bullet: "Introduced an AI-assisted weekly reporting process, cutting preparation time from 4 hours to 45 minutes."

8. Interview questions experienced candidates get

  • "Aren't you overqualified?" Explain why this role is the right next chapter, and how your experience shortens their ramp-up and reduces risk.
  • "Why are you changing now?" Keep it forward-looking: what you want to build next, never complaints about the past.
  • "How do you keep your skills current?" Give a specific, recent example, such as the AI project above.
  • "What are your salary expectations?" Give a researched range based on the role, not your last package, and say you're flexible for the right fit.
